The costing model

The anatomy of a composite pipe price

Strip a composite pipe price to its parts and you get a chain where every link is a table or a formula — geometry, process and commercial terms in one calculation:

price / m = ( material(DN × wall) + winding & cure(pressure class) )
             × liner multiplier(resin type)
             + fittings + hydrotest + packing
             + SG&A fees % → cost → + margin % → price
  • Material is geometry-driven. Resin and glass consumption per metre comes from diameter × wall matrices — one per product family — with liner resin, structural layers and optional barrier foils costed separately.
  • Machine cost is process-driven. Winding and cure time depend on pressure class and line speed; multiply by the press-line rate and you have conversion cost per metre.
  • Operations are per-unit adders. Heat treatment, tool cleaning, end fittings, hydrotest, packing — each a priced step, some flat, some dimension-dependent.
  • The commercial layer is contract-driven. SG&A allocation, fees and margin percentages applied on cost; plant origin, customer segment and currency change which tables apply.
Why it breaks

Where spreadsheet costing models fail

Every composite pipe maker has this file: decades of tables, nested formulas, plant variants, and exactly one person who understands it. The math is fine — the governance is not:

  • Interdependent tables multiply silently. Diameter × wall per product family, cure times per class, plant-specific rates, fitting models — edit one cell and a dozen prices shift with no trace.
  • Variants fork the file. Per-plant, per-segment, per-currency copies of the same workbook drift apart until nobody knows which is authoritative.
  • Quotes can't be replayed. A price from six months ago can't be explained — which table version, which resin price, which margin was live that day?

Pricera replaces the file without changing the math: the same tables and formulas become a versioned model, reference cases prove every edit reproduces known prices before publish, and each quote pins the exact inputs that produced it.

FAQ

Composite pipe pricing, answered

How is composite pipe priced?

Per metre, from a cost build-up: material cost driven by diameter and wall construction (liner resin, structural glass and resin, optional barrier foils), plus machine time for winding and cure driven by pressure class and line speed, plus per-unit operations such as end fittings, hydrotest and packing — then SG&A allocation and margin. Each element is typically a table or a formula, not a catalogue price.

What is a diameter × wall matrix in pipe costing?

A diameter × wall matrix maps two dimensions — nominal diameter (DN) against wall thickness or pressure class — to a material cost or quantity per metre. For example, material cost for DN600 at 12 mm wall is read from the matrix rather than computed from first principles each time.

Why is composite pipe pricing hard to run in spreadsheets?

The model is a web of interdependent tables: diameter × wall matrices per product family, cure and winding times per pressure class, liner multipliers, plant-specific rates, plus fittings priced in separate models that roll into one quote. A spreadsheet can hold the numbers but cannot govern them — no version pinning per quote, no test that a table edit reproduces yesterday's price, and plant, customer segment and currency variants multiply the file copies.

Can a pricing model handle pipe assemblies — pipe plus fittings?

Yes, when the engine supports nested models. Each fitting type is its own costing model; the pipe quote assembles the pipe line plus the fitting lines, sharing the same customer, currency and margin terms — and every component line carries its own cost breakdown into the quote total.
